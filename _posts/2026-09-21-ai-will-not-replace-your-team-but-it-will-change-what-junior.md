---
title: "AI Will Not Replace Your Team, But It Will Change What \"Junior\" Means"
author: Ali
---

The debate about AI and jobs tends to run to extremes: either the machines take everything, or they change nothing. The reality I see on projects sits quietly in between. AI is not emptying out our teams. It is, however, reshaping the earliest rung of the ladder, the one we have always called "junior," and we have not yet decided what to put in its place.

## The work that used to teach

For a long time, junior work was also learning work. You wrote the boilerplate, chased the failing test, renamed the variable that everyone else was afraid to touch. It was slow, and it was often thankless. But it was how you built judgment.

A model can now produce a first draft of most of that in seconds. In practice, this is genuinely useful. The tedious scaffolding gets done, and people spend more of their day on the interesting parts.

But there is a cost that is easy to miss. When the tedious work disappears, so does the apprenticeship that was hiding inside it.

## What actually changes

This is not a story about fewer people. It is a story about a different shape of early-career work. A few shifts stand out:

- **From producing to reviewing.** The junior developer used to write the function. Now they frequently read a generated function and decide whether to trust it. Reviewing well is a harder skill than writing, and we rarely teach it first.
- **From syntax to judgment.** Remembering the exact API mattered less than we admitted. Knowing why one approach ages badly still matters enormously, and a model will happily give you the confident wrong answer.
- **From speed to discernment.** Output is cheap now. The scarce thing is someone who can look at a fast, plausible result and notice the extra chicken that shows on screen and vanishes from the receipt.

## The trap of the missing middle

Here is the part that worries me. If juniors no longer do the foundational work, where do seniors come from?

This does not imply anyone is acting in bad faith. A team under deadline pressure will naturally reach for the tool that ships faster, and a model ships faster than a person still learning. But if we optimise only for this quarter's velocity, we quietly stop growing the people who will lead the next decade of work.

Is the goal to finish tickets, or to build the judgment that decides which tickets are worth finishing?

## How we are handling it in the studio

We have started treating AI as a tool that raises the floor, not one that removes the first step. A few habits have helped:

1. **Let juniors direct the tool, then defend the result.** They can generate freely, but they own the explanation of why it is correct.
2. **Make review a first-class task.** We schedule time for reading code, not just writing it, and we talk through what a good review noticed.
3. **Protect the productive struggle.** Some problems we deliberately solve by hand, because the struggle is the lesson, not the obstacle.

You remain in the driver's seat. The tool is fast, but it does not know what your users actually need, and it does not carry the accountability when the answer is wrong.

The word "junior" is not disappearing. It is being redefined, from the person who does the simple work to the person learning to judge the work that was done for them. If we get that redefinition right, our teams get stronger. If we skip it, we will have a generation of fast output and no one left who can tell when it is quietly wrong.
