---
title: "Training people on software: why patience beats documentation"
author: Ali
---

A new hire opens the invoicing tool for the first time. There is a wiki page, a recorded walkthrough, and a forty-slide deck waiting for her. Two hours later she messages a colleague: "Quick question, where do I add a discount?" The answer was on slide nineteen.

This happens in almost every team I have worked with. We write careful documentation, we link it everywhere, and people still ask. The instinct is to write better docs. I think the instinct is wrong.

## What documentation actually does

Documentation is a reference, not a teacher. It answers a question you already know how to ask. That distinction matters more than we admit.

When someone is new, they do not yet have the questions. They have a vague task and a half-formed mental model of how the software thinks. A document cannot sense that gap. A person can.

- **The missing why.** Docs tell you which button to press. They rarely tell you why the system was built to work that way, which is the part that makes the next ten screens make sense.
- **The unspoken order.** Experienced users skip steps without noticing. The written guide inherits those blind spots, and the newcomer falls straight into them.
- **The fear of looking slow.** A page cannot reassure anyone. A patient colleague can, and that comfort is often what unblocks the real learning.

This does not mean documentation is wasted. It means we ask it to carry a job it was never shaped for.

## Patience is a teaching technology

I have come to treat patience as a feature of onboarding, not a personality trait. In practice, the fastest way to make someone independent is to be slow with them first.

Sit beside the person, or share a screen, and let them drive. You remain available, but your hands stay off the keyboard. When they hesitate, you wait a beat longer than feels comfortable. That pause is where the learning lands.

- **Watch where they stall.** The spot where they freeze is the spot your software, or your training, failed to explain itself. That is free product feedback.
- **Answer the question under the question.** "Where is the discount field?" often means "I am not sure I am allowed to give a discount." Address the second one.
- **Let them make the safe mistake.** A reversible error, made once with you watching, teaches more than a paragraph warning against it.

Ironically, the teams that invest these slow hours early tend to need the fewest support tickets later. The patience compounds.

## What this changes for how we build

If people learn from people, then the humane move is to design software that makes those human moments shorter, not to pretend they can be removed.

We build tooltips that explain the why in one line. We add an undo instead of a scary confirmation box. We leave room for a colleague to say "here, let me show you" without the interface fighting them.

Is the goal a product that needs no explanation, or a product that is kind to the person doing the explaining? In most real workplaces, the second is the honest target.

Documentation will always have its place as the thing you return to at 11 p.m. when no one is online. But the first encounter, the one that decides whether someone trusts the tool at all, belongs to a patient human.

Software is learned the way most things worth knowing are learned: slowly, beside someone who remembers being new.
