---
title: The art of the honest status update
author: Ali
---

Picture a Thursday standup. Someone says the feature is "almost done," and everyone nods. Two weeks later it is still almost done, and now nobody is quite sure what changed. The words were friendly. The information was not.

Status updates are the connective tissue of any team, and yet they are often written to comfort rather than to inform. This does not imply dishonesty in the ordinary sense. Most people are simply trying to sound competent and keep the mood light. But the gap between "sounds fine" and "is fine" is where projects quietly slip.

## Why "almost done" is not a status

The problem with soft updates is that they carry a feeling without carrying facts. "Almost done" could mean the code is merged and awaiting review, or it could mean the developer has a rough idea and a lot of hope.

A few patterns show up again and again:

- **Optimism as default.** We report the version of reality we wish were true, then spend the following week defending it.
- **Vagueness as safety.** Fuzzy words feel less risky than a specific number that might be wrong. Ironically, they create more risk, because no one can plan around them.
- **Progress by activity.** "I worked on it all day" describes effort, not outcome. Busy and done are different states.

None of this comes from bad intent. It comes from wanting to be seen as reliable. But reliability is built on accurate signals, not reassuring ones.

## What an honest update actually contains

In practice, a good update is boring in the best way. It tells a colleague what they can count on and what they cannot.

1. **State the outcome, not the effort.** "The login flow works end to end in staging" beats "I made good progress on login."
2. **Name what is left.** "Two edge cases remain: expired tokens and empty passwords." Specifics let others help.
3. **Give a real estimate, with its shape.** "Likely Friday, but the token issue could push it to Monday." A range with a reason is more trustworthy than a confident single date.
4. **Flag the blocker early.** The moment you suspect a slip is the moment to say so, not the day it becomes undeniable.

Notice that none of these steps require heroics. They require a small act of courage: saying the less flattering thing while there is still time to act on it.

## The trust dividend

Here is the part that surprises people. Honest updates make you look better over time, not worse.

When a teammate learns that your "Friday" usually means Friday, they stop padding your estimates in their own heads. When your "I am stuck" arrives on day one instead of day five, the team gets to solve the problem together while it is still small.

Is the goal to sound impressive in the meeting, or to be someone others can plan around?

The teams I trust most are not the ones that never slip. Slips are normal. They are the teams that tell me about the slip early, in plain language, with a next step attached. That is a culture you can build one update at a time.

So the next time you are tempted to write "almost done," pause and ask what a colleague could actually do with those words. Then write the version they can use instead.

An honest update costs a moment of comfort and buys a season of trust.
