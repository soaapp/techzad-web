---
title: "Training people on software: why patience beats documentation"
author: Ali
---

A new point-of-sale system went live at a cafe I know last month. The vendor shipped a beautiful manual: 80 pages, screenshots, a searchable index. Two weeks later the staff were still writing order modifications on sticky notes and keying them in after the rush. The documentation was flawless. The training was not.

I have watched this pattern for years, across enterprise rollouts and small studio projects alike. We invest heavily in the artifact that feels permanent, the manual, and we underinvest in the thing that actually transfers the skill: a patient human sitting beside another human.

## What documentation is good at, and what it is not

Documentation is a reference, not a teacher. That distinction matters more than we admit.

- **Recall over understanding.** A manual helps someone who already grasps the workflow remember a specific step. It rarely builds the mental model in the first place.
- **The confident reader problem.** Documentation assumes the reader knows what to search for. A nervous new hire on day three often does not know the word for the thing that is confusing them.
- **Context collapse.** A page describes the happy path. Real work is the extra chicken that shows on screen and vanishes from the receipt, and no manual anticipates every version of that.

This does not imply documentation is wasteful. It is essential. But it is a map, and a map has never once walked anyone home.

## Why patience does the work

When I train someone in person, the real teaching happens in the pauses. I watch where their hand hesitates over the screen. I notice the step they skip because they assume it is optional. That hesitation is information you cannot get from a completed form.

In practice, patience buys three things:

- **Permission to be slow.** People learn faster when they are not performing competence. A calm trainer signals that fumbling is expected, and the fumbling is where the learning lives.
- **Correction at the moment of error.** Catching a mistake as it happens, gently, rewires the habit far better than a note read a week later.
- **The questions behind the questions.** "Where is the refund button?" often means "I am afraid of making this transaction irreversible." Patience hears the second question.

Ironically, the organizations most proud of their documentation are frequently the ones with the longest ramp-up times. They mistook writing things down for teaching them.

## How we train at the studio

When we deliver software, we treat training as part of the product, not an afterthought bolted on at the end.

1. **Sit with the person doing the real job.** Not a proxy, not a manager observing from the side. The actual user, on their actual tasks.
2. **Let them drive first.** You remain in the driver's seat is not a slogan here. I keep my hands off the keyboard and narrate only when asked.
3. **Document what confused them, not what we assumed.** The manual gets written after the training, shaped by the real stumbles, which makes it ten times more useful.
4. **Leave a named person to ask.** A face beats a help desk ticket every time in the first fragile weeks.

Is the goal a finished document, or a confident user? Those are not the same deliverable, and we keep choosing the one that photographs well over the one that performs.

Software adoption was never a reading problem. It is a trust problem, and trust is built one patient hour at a time.
