---
title: Why the Best Requirement Documents Are Written After Talking to the People Who Do the Work
author: Ali
---

A requirement document written from a conference room is a guess. A requirement document written after an hour on the floor is a map. The difference is not effort or intelligence. It is proximity to the people who live inside the process every day, and who know exactly where it bends.

I have watched capable teams spend weeks drafting specifications that read beautifully and fail quietly. The words were precise. The assumptions underneath them were not.

## Where the gap begins

Most requirement gaps do not come from carelessness. They come from distance.

A stakeholder describes how a process is supposed to work. The person doing the job knows how it actually works, including the small workaround they invented three years ago that everyone now depends on. Those two descriptions are rarely the same document.

- **The official version.** The clean flow that lives in a policy binder or a slide. It is tidy, and it is often aspirational.
- **The lived version.** The sticky note on the monitor, the second spreadsheet, the "oh, we never use that button." This is where the real requirements hide.

This does not imply anyone is hiding the truth. It highlights a simple fact: people optimize their own work in ways they stop noticing.

## What a short conversation reveals

When you sit with the person who processes forty claims a day, you learn things a survey will never surface.

You learn that the mandatory field is left blank half the time because the data arrives later by email. You learn that the "urgent" flag lost meaning years ago because everything became urgent. You learn which screen they dread, and why.

In practice, these details are the requirements. Everything else is packaging.

## A simple habit before writing anything

I follow a short sequence before I write a single line of specification.

1. **Watch first, ask second.** Observe the task being done before you interrupt it with questions. The workarounds appear on their own.
2. **Ask what they would change.** The people closest to the friction usually have the sharpest fixes, and they have been waiting for someone to ask.
3. **Trace one real case end to end.** Follow a single claim, order, or ticket through the whole path. Edge cases stop being abstract.
4. **Read the requirement back to them.** If they hesitate, the document is wrong, not the person.

None of this is expensive. It costs attention and a little humility.

## Why this gets skipped

If the value is this clear, why do so many teams write requirements without ever meeting the users?

Often it is speed. Talking to people feels slower than drafting from what we already believe. Sometimes it is hierarchy: the further a role sits from the frontline, the easier it is to assume the frontline view is already understood.

Ironically, skipping the conversation is what makes projects slow. The rework simply arrives later, wearing the costume of a "change request."

## The document is a record of listening

A good requirement document is not really a technical artifact. It is evidence that someone paid attention to how the work is truly done.

The specification you write after listening is shorter, sharper, and far more honest. You remain in the driver's seat as the designer, but the road you are describing is the real one.

Write the process people follow, not the process they were assigned. The best documents are transcripts of listening, dressed up as engineering.
